Texto: If things haven't felt crazy enough, the government has now told families to prepare and keep three critical things at home. My brother who is former special forces told me to add a fourth.

I'm Jen. I'm 44. I live just outside Pensacola, Florida, with my husband Mike, our two kids, and a golden retriever who thinks the couch belongs to her.

Maddie is nine. Sam is six. Our life is school drop-offs, soccer practice, and a fridge covered in drawings nobody is allowed to throw away.

I'm not a prepper. I want to say that clearly, because I know how some of this is going to sound.

I don't watch end-of-the-world videos. I've never owned a bucket of freeze-dried anything. Until this month, our emergency plan was a flashlight in the junk drawer that may or may not have had batteries in it.

My brother Dan is the sensible one in our family. He moved to London twelve years ago for work. He lives there now with his wife and their two boys, and he works in insurance.

Dan reads the fine print on everything. He checks the tire pressure before every road trip. But he is not a worrier. He's the one who tells everyone else to calm down.

He thinks people who talk about the end of the world need a hobby.

So when he sent me a link on a Sunday night in late September, with no message at all, I almost didn't open it.

It was an interview with Britain's Defence Secretary.

The man in charge of Britain's entire military had sat down with one of their biggest newspapers. And he'd told ordinary families to keep three things in the house.

Some cash.

A supply of bottled water.

And some tinned food. Canned food, to us. The kind you can eat cold, "if your electric goes," as he put it.

He listed the reasons, one after another. Cyber attacks. Power cuts. Flooding. Wildfires. The wars in Ukraine and the Middle East.

He said he wasn't trying to scare anyone. He said most people would have these things in the cupboard already. His exact words were "alert, not alarmed."

I read it twice, then texted Dan back. "That's a British thing," I wrote. "Different country, different problems."

He replied with one line. "It's not a British thing. Please just do it."

I didn't do it. I put a little heart on his message, made the kids' lunches for Monday, and forgot about it.

I want you to remember that part, because it's the part I think about most now.

Two weeks later, a storm called Isaias started building in the Gulf.

At first it looked like every other storm we get this time of year. The weather people drew the cone on the map. The kids asked if school would be closed. Mike went to fill up the car.

Then it got stronger. By Friday it was a hurricane, and it was heading for our stretch of coast.

Isaias came ashore near Destin on the night of Friday, October 9th, as a Category 2. We're not far from there.

I'll spare you most of that night. The wind sounds different when it's that strong. It doesn't howl. It roars, like a train that never finishes passing.

The kids slept in our bed, and the dog wouldn't leave the hallway. Around midnight, the power went.

By the next morning, more than 880,000 homes and businesses across Florida, Alabama and Georgia had no electricity. We were one of them.

Nobody I know got through that storm without a story. Four people didn't get through it at all.

I'm not going to pretend we had it worst. We didn't. Our roof held. Nobody was hurt.

But something happened that weekend that I wasn't ready for, and it's the reason I'm writing this.

It was the water.

I'd always thought of a hurricane as a wind problem. Board up the windows, bring in the patio chairs, wait it out.

I had never once thought of it as a water problem.

On Saturday the tap was still running. I filled the kettle without thinking, the way I always do, and then remembered the stove was electric.

On Sunday, the notice came through on a neighbor's phone, because ours were nearly dead. Boil water before drinking it. Our town was under a boil-water advisory.

Boil it with what, I thought.

Mike dug the camping stove out of the garage. It had one canister of gas left, about half full.

That's when I actually counted what we had.

Most of a case of the little bottles. A gallon jug in the fridge, already going warm. A couple of sports drinks the kids had forgotten about in the pantry.

Maybe three gallons. For four people and a dog. In the Florida heat. With no air conditioning.

We went through the first gallon by lunchtime. I wasn't even counting the dog's bowl.

Mike drove to the store on Sunday afternoon. The roads were full of branches and the traffic lights were out. He came home an hour later with nothing.

"No water," he said. "Not one case. People were buying sparkling water and juice and anything else that was wet."

I remember standing in the kitchen in the heat, looking at the bottles lined up on the counter, and doing a kind of math I'd never done before in my life.

Not money math. How many days.

Sam asked for a glass of water before bed that night. I gave him half a cup and told him to sip it slowly.

He's six, and he didn't understand why. I didn't want to explain it.

That night, with the kids finally asleep and the whole house silent, I opened Dan's message again.

"Please just do it."

I called him the next morning. In London it was already the afternoon.

"Are you okay?" was the first thing he said, because he'd been watching the storm on the news all weekend.

I told him we were fine. I told him about the water. I told him about the half cup.

He was quiet for a second. Then he said, "Did you do the three things?"

"No," I said. "I didn't think it applied to us."

"I did all three," he said. "And one more."

I asked him why. What made a man who rolls his eyes at everything take a politician's advice so seriously.

He told me it wasn't really the politician. It was that governments were all starting to say the same thing at the same time.

The European Union has been telling families to keep 72 hours of food and water at home since last year. Britain is launching a whole national campaign about it this autumn.

"When governments start telling people to keep water in the house," he said, "they're not guessing. They're planning. They just don't want to say it any louder than they have to."

"It isn't about war," he said. "It's about everything going wrong at once. A storm. A power cut. A cyber attack on the wrong system. You don't need the end of the world. You just need one bad week."

"And you just had yours," he said.

Then he explained the part I'd never understood.

Our tap water runs on electricity. Pumps push it through the pipes to every house. Treatment plants clean it. Computers control both.

When the power goes, the pumps can go with it. When the pumps go, the pressure drops. And when the pressure drops, dirty water can get into the pipes.

That's why the advisories come after storms. It isn't always a broken pipe. Sometimes it's just the power.

He sent me a story from the week before. Over on the other side of Florida, a storm knocked out power to the water pumps, the pressure dropped, and families were told to boil their water before drinking it.

No power. No pumps. No safe water. That's the whole chain, and it's only three links long.

Then he asked me a question I still think about.

"How long would your water have lasted if that advisory had gone on for two weeks?"

I didn't need the math. We'd barely made it through two days.

"Okay," he said. "Here's the number they don't put in the headline."

The British government's own guidance says a person needs 2.5 to 3 liters of water a day just to survive. That's drinking water only.

To drink, cook and keep clean, it says about 10 liters per person, per day.

We're a family of four. That's 40 liters a day. About ten and a half gallons. Every single day.

For three days, that's 120 liters. Over 30 gallons. Roughly five cases of gallon jugs stacked in the garage, just to get through a long weekend.

My three gallons wouldn't have lasted us until lunch on day one by that standard.

"That's the problem with bottled water," Dan said. "It's a countdown. Every bottle you open is one fewer. You can fill the garage and all you've done is buy a few more days. Then you're right back where you started."

He walked me through it the way he walks through everything. Like an insurance claim.

Option one. Buy more bottled water. It helps, and you should have some. But it's heavy, it fills the garage, and you can't take it with you if you have to leave. A week of water for a family our size weighs about 600 pounds.

Option two. Boil it. That works, as long as the power is on or the gas is on. Which is exactly the moment you don't have them.

Option three. Go to the store. We'd tried that.

"So what's the fourth thing?" I asked him.

He laughed. "I wondered when you'd ask. It isn't more water. It's a way to make more."

He keeps a small hollow-fiber water filter in the kitchen drawer. One in the car. One in each of his boys' school bags. Each one about the size of a flashlight.

"The three things get you through a few days," he said. "This is what you need when a few days isn't enough."

I'll be honest, my first reaction was the same as it had been to the article. A filter? Like the camping ones? So I sat down and read. The good portable filters use something called a hollow-fiber membrane.

Picture thousands of tiny straws packed side by side, each one thinner than a hair. The walls of those little straws are full of microscopic holes.

The holes are about 0.1 microns wide. That's so small that bacteria and parasites physically can't fit through. They get trapped on the outside. Clean water passes through to the inside.

To give you an idea of how small that is, a single E. coli bacterium is roughly one to two microns long. Giardia, a parasite that causes a lot of the stomach illness people get from untreated water, is around ten microns across. That's about a hundred times wider than the holes it would need to squeeze through.

It isn't a chemical killing anything. It's more like a sieve so fine that the things that make you sick simply can't get to the other side.

No chemicals. No electricity. No batteries. Just the size of the hole.

I asked Dan the obvious question. We already have a filter on the fridge, and a pitcher filter on the counter. Doesn't that do the same thing?

It doesn't. Most of those are carbon filters. They're designed to make tap water that's already been treated taste better, by taking out chlorine and odors. Most of them aren't designed to stop bacteria and parasites, and the fridge one doesn't work at all when the power's off.

That one honestly surprised me. I'd assumed we were already covered.

It's the same kind of technology used in field water filtration by aid organizations. The people who show up after floods and earthquakes, when the taps in a whole region have stopped.

You can drink straight through one like a straw. Or you can attach it to a bottle and squeeze the water through.

Which means almost any freshwater you can get to becomes drinking water. Rain in a bucket. A creek. A pond. The water still sitting in your pipes and your water heater.

I thought about that weekend. The rain barrel by Mike's shed was full to the top. The creek behind the kids' school was running high. We'd been surrounded by water the whole time.

We just couldn't drink any of it.

Using one is simpler than I expected. You fill the bag or bottle with water from wherever you are, screw the filter on, and squeeze or sip. When it starts to slow down, you push a little clean water back through it with a syringe to flush it out, and it flows again.

There's nothing to set up and nothing to plug in. Dan's youngest figured it out in about ten seconds, which tells you everything.

I also wanted to know what these filters can't do, because I don't trust anything that claims to do everything.

A filter like this is built for freshwater. It removes bacteria and parasites. It does not remove viruses, chemicals, or salt. It won't make seawater or a chemical spill safe. And if your water company tells you to boil, you still do what they say.

Honestly, that made me trust it more. It does one job. The exact job I needed done that weekend.

And it cost less than our weekly grocery run.

Mike, I should say, rolled his eyes at all of this at first. Same as I had in September. He thinks I've been reading too much.

Then I reminded him about the store on Sunday afternoon. The empty shelves. The half cup.

He went quiet. The next day he asked me to order one for his truck.

One filter in a drawer doesn't count down. It just keeps going, for as long as there's freshwater to put through it.

That's the fourth thing. Not more storage. A way out of the countdown.

One in the kitchen drawer, next to the flashlight that now definitely has batteries in it.

One in the glovebox of each car.

One in each of the kids' backpacks.

And one in the hurricane box, which we now actually have.

The first weekend after they arrived, I took the kids out to the rain barrel by the shed.

Maddie filled a cup with rainwater. It had a leaf in it and a few bits floating around. She made a face at it.

Sam drank through the filter like it was a juice box. He said it tasted like nothing. Then he wanted to do it again.

They thought it was a science experiment. I let them think that.

Because the next time the power goes and the notice comes through, I don't want it to be the first time they've ever seen one. I want it to be normal.

I also did the three things. The cash is in an envelope in the safe. The canned food is on the bottom shelf of the pantry. And yes, we have bottled water now too.
